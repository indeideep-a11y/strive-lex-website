Operational Debt and the Section 8 Demand Notice Under the IBC

An operational creditor cannot go straight to the NCLT. This article explains how a Section 8 demand notice works, the ten-day window, and what makes it valid.

Category: Insolvency & Restructuring Last Reviewed: 27 October 2026
Please note: This article is general educational information, not legal advice on your specific facts. For guidance specific to your business, please book a consultation.

Before an operational creditor can ask the NCLT to start insolvency proceedings against a company, the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 requires a formal step first: a demand notice under Section 8. Getting this step wrong is one of the commonest reasons an otherwise genuine claim is delayed or rejected.

What counts as operational debt

Operational debt is a claim in respect of the provision of goods or services, including employment, or a debt in respect of dues arising under a law and payable to the Central Government, a State Government or a local authority. A supplier owed money for goods delivered, or a service provider owed fees for work performed, is the typical operational creditor.

This is different from a financial debt, which involves money disbursed against the time value of money, such as a loan. The distinction decides which section of the Code applies and how much a creditor must prove.

The demand notice

Under Section 8, an operational creditor delivers a demand notice, or a copy of the invoice demanding payment, to the corporate debtor requiring payment of the unpaid operational debt. The notice must be in the form prescribed under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy (Application to Adjudicating Authority) Rules, 2016, and it must state the amount in default.

The notice should identify the debt clearly: the contract or purchase order, the invoices, the dates on which payment fell due, and the amount claimed, including any interest that the contract actually provides for. A vague or inflated notice invites a dispute about the figure itself.

The ten-day window

Once the corporate debtor receives the demand notice, it has ten days to respond. In that period it can do one of two things. It can pay the amount demanded, or it can send the operational creditor a notice of dispute, or show that a suit or arbitration on the same subject was already pending before the demand notice was received.

If the debtor does neither within the ten days, the operational creditor may file an application under Section 9 before the NCLT. The date of receipt matters, so the manner of delivery and proof of delivery should be treated as seriously as the contents of the notice.

What follows in the Section 9 application

The application must be accompanied by documents including the demand notice, proof of service, an affidavit that no notice of dispute has been received, and a statement from the operational creditor's bank confirming that no payment has been received. The default must also be at or above the statutory minimum, currently one crore rupees.

The NCLT is required to decide whether to admit or reject the application within fourteen days of receiving it, and where the application is incomplete the creditor is given a short period to cure the defect. Admission leads to the commencement of the Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process; rejection can follow if the debt is not proved or if there is a genuine dispute.

Practical points before you send the notice

First, check the paper trail. Delivery challans, acknowledgements, invoices and any admission of liability by the debtor are what the Tribunal will look at. Second, look for anything in the file that could be read as a dispute raised earlier, such as complaints about quality or short supply, because that is the debtor's most likely answer. Third, remember that the NCLT is not a debt-recovery forum. The Supreme Court has repeatedly cautioned that the Code is not meant to be used as a collection tool, so a creditor with a weak or contested claim may be better served by a civil or commercial suit.
