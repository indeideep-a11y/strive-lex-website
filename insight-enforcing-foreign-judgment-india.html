Enforcing a Foreign Judgment or Arbitral Award in India

The enforcement route depends heavily on one distinction that's easy to overlook early: whether the underlying decision is a court judgment or an arbitral award, and which country it came from.

Category: International Clients Last Reviewed: 3 August 2026
Please note: This article is general educational information, not legal advice on your specific facts. For guidance specific to your business, please book a consultation.

A client with a favourable judgment or arbitral award from another jurisdiction, and a counterparty or assets located in India, faces a genuinely different enforcement path depending on the nature of that decision. Understanding which route applies early avoids wasted time pursuing the wrong process.

Foreign Court Judgments: Reciprocating vs. Non-Reciprocating Territories

Under the Civil Procedure Code, 1908, a judgment from a foreign court in a country that India has formally declared a "reciprocating territory" can be enforced relatively directly, by filing execution proceedings in an Indian district court as though the judgment were an Indian decree. The list of reciprocating territories is limited to a specific set of countries — it does not include every major jurisdiction, and confirming whether the originating country is on that list is the essential first step.

For judgments from non-reciprocating territories, the judgment cannot be executed directly. Instead, the judgment itself becomes evidence in a fresh civil suit filed in India, where the Indian court examines the judgment on limited specified grounds (such as whether it was given by a court of competent jurisdiction, on the merits, and not obtained by fraud) before effectively re-establishing it as an enforceable decision. This is a materially longer and more resource-intensive route than direct execution.

Foreign Arbitral Awards Are Generally More Straightforward

India is a signatory to the New York Convention, and foreign arbitral awards from other Convention countries are generally enforceable under Part II of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, through a more streamlined process than a non-reciprocating-territory court judgment. Indian courts have grounds on which enforcement can be resisted (similar to the Convention's own limited grounds — issues of public policy, due process, or the award exceeding the scope of the arbitration agreement), but the presumption favours enforcement rather than re-litigation of the merits.

Practical Steps Before Filing

Before initiating enforcement proceedings, it's worth confirming: the specific Indian assets or the counterparty's presence in India that make enforcement viable in the first place (a judgment is only useful if there's something to enforce it against), whether the award or judgment has been properly authenticated and, where required, apostilled or legalised for use in India, and whether any parallel proceedings exist that could affect the enforcement strategy.

Why Early Local Counsel Involvement Matters Here Specifically

Enforcement strategy in India benefits significantly from local counsel involvement before filing, not after — identifying the correct forum, confirming reciprocating-territory status, and structuring the application correctly the first time avoids delays that are considerably harder to recover from once a flawed filing is already before a court.

If you're assessing whether a foreign judgment or award your client holds can be enforced in India, the reciprocating-territory question and the judgment-versus-award distinction are the two things worth establishing first — they determine almost everything else about how the matter proceeds.
