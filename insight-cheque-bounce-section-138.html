Cheque Bounce Cases in India: What Section 138 Actually Means

A dishonoured cheque isn't just a banking problem — for the person who issued it, it can become a criminal case. Here's how the process actually works.

Category: Litigation & Dispute Resolution Last Reviewed: 3 August 2026
Please note: This article is general educational information, not legal advice on your specific facts. For guidance specific to your business, please book a consultation.

Most people treat a bounced cheque as an embarrassing inconvenience to be sorted out with an apology and a fresh payment. Under Indian law, it can be considerably more serious than that. Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 makes the dishonour of a cheque, issued in discharge of a legally enforceable debt, a criminal offence — not merely a civil one.

What Actually Triggers Section 138

The offence applies specifically when a cheque is returned unpaid because of insufficient funds, or because the amount exceeds what's arranged with the bank, and the cheque was issued to discharge a genuine debt or liability. A cheque given as a gift, or one that bounces for a purely technical reason unrelated to funds (a signature mismatch, for instance), doesn't automatically fall under this section in the same way.

The Notice Requirement — and Why the Timeline Matters

The payee cannot simply file a criminal complaint the moment a cheque bounces. The law requires a demand notice to be sent to the drawer within 30 days of receiving the bank's dishonour memo, demanding payment within 15 days of the notice. Only if payment isn't made within that 15-day window does a cause of action for a criminal complaint arise — and the complaint itself must then be filed within one month of that window closing. Missing any of these windows can be fatal to an otherwise valid claim, which is why acting promptly after a bounce matters more than most people realise.

What's at Stake

A conviction under Section 138 can result in imprisonment of up to two years, a fine that may extend to twice the cheque amount, or both. In practice, many cases settle before trial — courts actively encourage compounding of the offence — but the criminal exposure is real and gives the payee genuine leverage that a purely civil recovery suit wouldn't.

For the Person Who Issued the Cheque

If you're on the receiving end of a Section 138 notice, the existence of a genuine, provable dispute about the underlying debt is a real defence — but it needs to be substantiated, not simply asserted. Ignoring the notice, on the assumption that "it's just a cheque bounce," is one of the more common and costly mistakes we see, precisely because of how firm the statutory timelines are.

Whether you're the one owed money or the one facing a notice, the window to act correctly is narrower than most people expect. If you're dealing with either side of a bounced cheque, timing your response correctly is often the difference between a quick resolution and a prolonged one.
