What Actually Happens If You Ignore a Legal Notice

It's not a court order, so nothing happens immediately — but that doesn't mean ignoring it is a safe strategy. Here's what's actually at stake.

Category: Litigation & Dispute Resolution Last Reviewed: 3 August 2026
Please note: This article is general educational information, not legal advice on your specific facts. For guidance specific to your business, please book a consultation.

Receiving a legal notice is unsettling, and a common instinct is to either panic or, at the opposite extreme, dismiss it entirely on the theory that "it's just a letter from a lawyer, not a court order." Neither reaction reflects what a legal notice actually is, or what ignoring one actually costs.

What a Legal Notice Actually Is

A legal notice is a formal communication, usually sent by an advocate on a client's behalf, stating a grievance and demanding a specific remedy within a stated timeframe. It carries no automatic legal force by itself — it doesn't compel payment, and it isn't a judgment. Its function is to put you formally on notice of a claim, and in many cases, it's a mandatory precondition before the sender can proceed to a formal legal process at all.

Why Ignoring It Is Rarely the Safe Option

Several real consequences flow from silence, even though none are immediate or automatic. First, in many types of proceedings — including cheque bounce cases under Section 138 and various consumer and commercial disputes — a notice and the recipient's failure to respond within the stated window is itself a documented part of the record a court will later see, and an unanswered notice reads differently to a judge than a promptly and reasonably contested one. Second, some notices carry statutory time limits (as with Section 138) where the sender's own right to act depends on a specific window passing without resolution — meaning your silence can actually accelerate the sender's ability to escalate. Third, and most overlooked: a notice you fail to respond to at all forecloses the opportunity to put your own version of events on record early, while a considered response — even a firm one disputing the claim — creates a documented position you can point back to later.

What a Considered Response Actually Achieves

Responding doesn't mean capitulating. A well-drafted reply can dispute the claim entirely, dispute specific facts while conceding others, propose a resolution, or simply record your position clearly for the future. In many disputes we've seen, a prompt, well-reasoned response to a notice materially changed the trajectory of what followed — sometimes closing the matter outright, sometimes simply putting the sender on notice that the claim will be contested, which changes their calculus about whether litigation is worth pursuing.

If you've received a legal notice, treating it as neither an emergency nor as noise is usually the right instinct — it deserves a considered, timely response, ideally reviewed before that response goes out.
