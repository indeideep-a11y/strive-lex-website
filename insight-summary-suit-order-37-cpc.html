Summary Suits Under Order XXXVII CPC: A Faster Route to Recover Money

A summary suit lets a plaintiff recover a clear money claim without a full trial unless the defendant shows a real defence. Here is how Order XXXVII works.

Category: Litigation & Dispute Resolution Last Reviewed: 27 October 2026
Please note: This article is general educational information, not legal advice on your specific facts. For guidance specific to your business, please book a consultation.

Where a debt is clear and documented, a full civil trial can take years. Order XXXVII of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 offers a faster path, the summary suit, which puts the burden on the defendant to show that there is something genuinely worth trying.

What kinds of claims qualify

Order XXXVII applies to suits upon bills of exchange, hundis and promissory notes, and to suits in which the plaintiff seeks to recover a debt or liquidated demand in money, with or without interest, arising on a written contract, on an enactment where the sum sought is a fixed amount, or on a guarantee where the claim is for a fixed sum. The common thread is that the amount is certain, not something that needs to be worked out at trial.

The procedure is available in the High Courts and in other courts that the High Court has notified for this purpose, so the plaintiff should confirm that the court it files in is covered.

How the plaint is framed

The plaint must state that the suit is filed under Order XXXVII, must not include claims outside its scope, and must carry the specific inscription required by the Order. The documents that prove the debt, such as the written contract, invoices, cheques or acknowledgements, should be filed with the plaint, because the whole point of the procedure is that the claim stands on paper.

The defendant's position

The defendant does not file a written statement in the ordinary way. It must enter an appearance within ten days of service of the summons, after which the plaintiff serves a summons for judgment. The defendant then has ten days from that service to apply for leave to defend, supported by an affidavit setting out the defence.

If the defendant does not apply for leave, or the leave is refused, the plaintiff is entitled to a decree. If leave is granted, the suit proceeds as an ordinary suit, so the application is where the real contest takes place.

The test for leave to defend

The principles for deciding an application for leave to defend were set out by the Supreme Court in IDBI Trusteeship Services Ltd. v. Hubtown Ltd. (2016). In broad terms, where the defendant shows a substantial defence that raises a triable issue, leave is granted unconditionally. Where the defence appears doubtful or improbable, leave may be granted on conditions such as depositing the amount or furnishing security. Where the defence is plainly frivolous or sham, leave is refused.

The court looks at the substance and the supporting documents, not the label. A defence with no paper behind it is unlikely to succeed.

When a summary suit is the right tool

It works best for a clear, documented money claim: unpaid invoices under a written contract, a dishonoured instrument, or a guaranteed sum. It is a poor fit where the amount depends on accounts that are in genuine dispute, where the contract is oral or incomplete, or where the plaintiff needs other reliefs as well. In commercial disputes, parties should also keep the Section 12A mediation requirement in mind when choosing how to proceed.
